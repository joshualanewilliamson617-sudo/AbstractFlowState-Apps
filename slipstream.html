Slipstream

Draw anything, then put it in moving air.

How the physics works

The air is real fluid math. The tunnel is a grid of cells, and each cell holds nine packets of air density moving in different directions. Every tick they collide (relax toward balance) and stream to neighboring cells. This is the lattice Boltzmann method, and when you average it, it obeys the Navier–Stokes equations — the same equations that govern real air. Wakes, vortices and pressure are not animated; they emerge. A Smagorinsky turbulence model keeps fast, thin-air flows stable.

Drag is measured, not guessed. Every time a packet of air bounces off your drawing, the momentum it hands over is added up. That total is the force. The drag coefficient is Cd = drag ÷ (½ · ρ · U² · L), where U is air speed and L is how wide your shape looks to the oncoming air. A flat plate sits near 2, a cylinder near 1.3, a good airfoil far lower.

Every number on screen is in real units. You set the fluid, the object's real size, the wind speed, the material it is made of and the gravity. From those, the app works out how many metres one grid cell stands for and how many seconds one simulation tick lasts, then reports speeds in m/s (or km/h or mph), distances in metres, time in seconds and forces in newtons. Because the simulation is a 2D slice, forces are given per metre of span: a value of 12 N/m means a 2 metre length of that shape feels about 24 N. The clock shows real elapsed seconds, and the playback readout tells you whether you are watching at real speed or in slow motion, because fast air needs far more ticks per second than a browser can run.

Reynolds number is the key to scale. Re = U · L ÷ ν compares momentum to stickiness (viscosity ν). Two flows with the same shape and the same Re behave identically, whether it is a model in a tunnel or a full-size wing. That is why Cd and Re are shown here instead of meters per second. Low Re (under ~40) gives smooth, symmetric flow. Above ~50 the wake starts shedding vortices in a zig-zag street. Push higher and it turns chaotic.

Falling uses the rider's view. The camera travels with the object. Instead of moving the shape through still air, the air moves past it at the object's speed — physically identical (Galilean relativity). While it speeds up, the air also feels the opposite of that acceleration, which produces the extra "added mass" push real objects feel. Speed stops rising at terminal velocity, where drag equals weight minus buoyancy. Heavier (denser) or narrower shapes fall faster.

Zero gravity removes weight. The wind grabs the object and accelerates it until it moves with the air; relative speed and drag fade to zero. Throw it into the wind and watch drag brake it.

Honest limits. This is a 2D slice: your drawing is treated as an endlessly long extruded shape, so a circle behaves like a cylinder, not a ball. Air is modelled far below the speed of sound. A shape filling more than about 15% of the tunnel's width reads a little high on drag (blockage). And a grid this size cannot resolve every eddy of a very high Reynolds number flow: above roughly Re 20,000 the tunnel runs at the highest Reynolds number it can resolve and says so in the readouts. Drag coefficients for blunt shapes barely change across that range, so the numbers stay close to reality, but the sudden drag drop that real spheres and cylinders show near Re 300,000 will not appear here.
