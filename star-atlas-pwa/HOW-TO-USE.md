# Star Atlas — how to use it

The app teaches itself on first launch. This page is the same thing in writing,
for when you'd rather read than tap.

---

## The first thirty seconds

Open it, tap **Allow and begin**, say yes to motion and yes to location.
Hold the phone up. Turn around slowly. The sky on screen is the sky in front
of you.

That's the whole app. Everything below is extra.

---

## The controls

**Bottom right, the round target button.** Sky tracking, on and off. On, the
view follows your phone. Off, you drag around with your finger. Tap it whenever
you want to stop chasing the sky and just look around.

**Top right, the three little icons.**

| | What it does |
|---|---|
| Eye | **Looking** — the patch of sky you're pointing at. The normal one. |
| Circle with a cross | **Whole sky** — the entire dome overhead squashed into one round chart, horizon around the rim. Good for seeing how constellations sit relative to each other. |
| Folded map | **Star map** — forget where you're standing, roam the whole sphere. Good for planning. |

**Tap anything on screen.** It tells you what it is. If several things are
bunched up, small **‹ ›** arrows appear so you can step through them one at a
time. Pinch to zoom.

---

## The five tabs

**Sky** — where you are and when. Use your location, or pick a city, or type
coordinates. Change the date and time to any point you like: type `-1000` in
the year box and you're looking at 1000 BC. Time flow lets you run the sky
forward at an hour a second, or a day, or a month — the best way to watch
planets wander. Meteor showers are listed here with a **Go** button that jumps
you to peak night.

**Cultures** — twenty ways of joining the same stars. Modern, Greek, Babylonian,
Chinese, Egyptian, Norse, Maya, Navajo, Inuit, Maori and more. Pick one and it
offers to set the sky to the era it came from. Do that with Babylonian and watch
the pole star move — that's 3,000 years of the Earth wobbling on its axis.

**Draw** — tap it, then tap star to star. Every tap after the first pulls a
line. Tap **Name it** and it's saved. Your figure is tied to those actual stars,
so it rises and sets with them and shows up correctly on any date.

**Layers** — what's drawn: Milky Way, planets, deep sky, meteors, names,
horizon, grid. The magnitude slider sets how faint the stars go — turn it down
if things feel sluggish on an older phone. **Red light** is here too. Turn it on
outside; red light doesn't wreck your night vision, so you can look at the phone
and then still see the actual sky.

**Gallery** — your saved constellations. Sign them with a name and publish, or
add someone else's to your sky.

## Looking through the Earth

The sky doesn't stop at the horizon, and neither does the app. Everything below
your feet is still drawn, just dimmed — aim the phone at the ground and you are
looking through the planet at the far side of the sky.

Tap anything and it tells you which way to turn and how far down it is:
*"N 0&deg; &middot; 25.9&deg; down, through the Earth."* That's the Sun at midnight
in June from New York — due north, a quarter of the way to straight down. It is
also how you find a constellation that hasn't risen yet, or watch a planet you
missed set and keep going.

In **Whole sky** view the horizon is the bright circle. Everything inside it is
overhead; the shaded ring outside it is the underside, with straight-down
smeared around the outer edge.

Switch it off in **Layers &rarr; See through the Earth** if you'd rather the
ground stayed solid.

## The Earth under your feet

Aim at the ground and the planet is drawn there: real coastlines, in gold,
spread across everything below your horizon. Africa, Australia, Antarctica —
whichever happens to lie between you and the stars on the far side.

The geometry behind it is a tidy piece of arithmetic. Standing on a sphere,
**any place sits exactly half its distance below your horizon.** Somewhere 90
degrees of arc away is 45 degrees down. The antipode — the one spot directly
opposite you — is straight down, always. So looking 25 degrees below the
horizon means looking at ground 50 degrees of arc away.

Tap anything below the horizon and it tells you where your line of sight comes
back out: *"out through the ground near 1,314 km from Perth."*

### The little globe

Bottom left, a small Earth seen from outside, turned so you are facing the
viewer. You are the dot in the middle. The gold line is the way you are
pointing, and when you aim below the horizon a dashed line runs on through the
planet to where it emerges.

The night side is shaded, which tells you at a glance why the sky looks the way
it does. If the bright edge is crossing your dot, you are standing at sunrise
or sunset.

Switch it off in **Layers &rarr; Draw the Earth**. It needs *See through the
Earth* on, since there is nothing to draw if the ground is solid.

## If the view shakes

It shouldn't any more, but if it does: **Sky → Steadiness**.

- **Locked** (the default) holds dead still. Best for actually identifying things.
- **Balanced** splits the difference.
- **Quick** follows instantly and shows every tremor in your hand.

Below that is **Let the compass correct drift**. Turn it *off* and the view runs
purely on the gyroscope — perfectly smooth, zero shake, but it slowly wanders
over ten or twenty minutes and you re-align by hand. That is the steadiest the
app gets.

There is also a **padlock button** just above the round target button, which
appears while tracking. Tap it and your aim freezes where it is, so you can
study a patch of sky without holding the phone still. The sky keeps turning
underneath; only your pointing stops.

## If the sky looks turned the wrong way

Phone compasses are genuinely mediocre, and metal, magnets and car dashboards
all throw them off.

**Sky → Align on a bright object.** A crosshair appears with a row of the
brightest things currently up. Put the crosshair on one you can plainly see —
the Moon is easiest — and tap its name. It tells you how far off you were and
corrects it. This overrides everything else and it sticks.

Also worth doing: the figure-eight wave. Hold the phone and trace a big
sideways 8 in the air a few times. That's how the phone recalibrates its
magnetometer, and it helps in every compass app, not just this one.

## If it freezes, or the sky looks wrong

**The padlock, just above the round target button, freezes your aim.** It is
easy to hit by accident, and a frozen view looks exactly like a crashed app.
If the sky has stopped following the phone, check that button first — lit
means frozen. Tap it again.

**No location means no accuracy.** If you skipped the location prompt or denied
it, the app falls back to a saved spot or to New York, and everything is drawn
for the wrong place. Sky tab, *Use my location*. If nothing is asked, the
browser is remembering an earlier no: tap the icon left of the address bar and
reset the location permission.

**A phone with no motion permission sits perfectly still.** Nothing moves, no
error appears. Tap the round target button once and answer the prompt.

**If it feels sluggish rather than frozen**, drop the magnitude slider in
Layers a notch or two. Ten thousand stars plus the Earth is a lot for an older
phone to redraw sixty times a second, and dropping to magnitude 5.5 roughly
halves the work for very little lost from the sky.

## Things that trip people up

**Nothing happens when I hold the phone up.** On iPhone, tap the round target
button once. Apple requires a tap before it hands over the compass — it can't
be automatic.

**It says the wrong place.** Sky tab → *Use my location*. If nothing is asked,
the browser is remembering an earlier no: tap the icon left of the address bar
and reset the location permission.

**The sky is empty.** Check the date. If you wound it back and left it there,
you may be looking at daytime. Sky tab → **Now**.

**Nothing works at all and there's no prompt.** The app has to be on `https://`.
Opened from a folder on your computer, sensors are switched off by the browser
and there's nothing the app can do about it.

---

## Best first thing to do

Go outside after dark. Turn on red light mode. Point at the brightest thing you
can see and find out what it is. Then draw a line between two stars nobody has
connected before and give it a name.
