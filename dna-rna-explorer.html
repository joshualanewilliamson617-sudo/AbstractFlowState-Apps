The basics, in plain terms

Everything above is made of one repeating part. Once you see the part, the whole thing gets simple.

The repeating part is a nucleotide. Each one has three pieces: a sugar, a phosphate group, and a base. The sugar and phosphate of one nucleotide link to the sugar and phosphate of the next, over and over, making a long chain — that's the smooth rail you see winding through the model. The bases stick off that rail like teeth.

There are only four bases in play at a time, and their order along the chain is the information. Three bases in a row spell one amino acid; strings of those spell a protein. A gene is a stretch of that code long enough to build something useful. Your full set of it — the genome — runs about 3.2 billion base pairs, packed into 23 pairs of chromosomes inside almost every cell you have.

DNA is the archive. It's double-stranded, and the two strands are complements of each other: wherever one says A the other says T, wherever one says G the other says C. That redundancy is why a cell can copy it cleanly and why it can repair damage — the intact strand is the answer key for the broken one. The two rails run in opposite directions and twist around each other roughly once every ten and a half rungs, which is where the helix comes from.

RNA is the working copy. Same idea, three differences: its sugar is ribose instead of deoxyribose (one extra oxygen, which makes it more chemically reactive and much shorter-lived), it uses uracil where DNA uses thymine, and it's usually a single strand. Because it's single-stranded it folds back on itself into loops and pockets, and those shapes do things — some RNAs act as switches, scaffolds, or even catalysts, not just messages.

How information actually moves

Often called the central dogma. It is the through-line of the whole field.

Step one — replication

DNA copies itself

Before a cell divides it unzips its DNA and builds a matching partner for each strand, so both daughter cells get a complete set.

Step two — transcription

DNA is read into RNA

An enzyme opens a bubble in the helix, reads one strand, and builds a messenger RNA copy of a single gene. Press "Transcribe" on the model to watch this.

Step three — translation

RNA is built into protein

A ribosome walks the mRNA three bases at a time while transfer RNAs deliver the matching amino acids. The finished protein does the work.

Messenger RNA isn't the only kind. Transfer RNA fetches amino acids. Ribosomal RNA forms the structural core of the ribosome itself. And a large class of non-coding RNAs — microRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, circular RNAs — never become protein at all; they regulate which genes run and how hard. That last group is most of what's driving the field right now.

Where this sits academically. The home discipline is molecular biology, with molecular genetics as the sub-area. It borders biochemistry (the chemistry of these molecules), genetics (inheritance), genomics (reading whole genomes at scale), structural biology (their 3D shapes), bioinformatics (the computing needed to handle the data), and synthetic biology plus genetic engineering on the applied side. All of it sits under biology. You'd meet it first in high-school biology, again in an introductory college biology course, and seriously in biochemistry or molecular biology.

Where the field is right now

Seven things that have changed in the last couple of years. Sources are linked under each — worth clicking, because this area moves fast enough that any snapshot goes stale.

RNA therapeutics

RNA stopped being a bit player

For fifty years RNA was taught as the courier between DNA and protein. That framing is gone. It's now treated as an active, programmable component of cell biology and a drug class in its own right — the industry has taken to calling this stretch an "RNAissance." The mRNA vaccines were the public proof; the deeper shift is that you can design an RNA sequence on a computer and have a working therapeutic far faster than you can develop a conventional small-molecule drug.

Sources: BIO 2026 · Nature RNA hub
Gene editing

CRISPR moved past cutting

The original CRISPR-Cas9 worked by slicing both strands of DNA and letting the cell's messy repair machinery patch the gap. The newer tools don't cut all the way through. Base editors chemically convert one letter into another in place. Prime editors write a short specified sequence directly. CRISPR-associated transposases can drop whole cargo sequences at a chosen spot. The practical effect is that a far wider range of disease-causing mutations is now addressable, with less collateral damage.

Source: Genes, June 2026
Clinical

Editing is in patients, and increasingly inside the body

The first approved CRISPR therapy cleared regulators in 2023 for sickle cell disease and beta thalassemia, and blood disorders still lead the Phase 3 pipeline. Roughly 250 gene-editing trials are being tracked, with over 150 active, spanning cancer, autoimmune disease and inherited conditions. The bigger change is the shift from ex vivo work — pull cells out, edit them, put them back — toward editing organs in place: one in-body trial reported a single dose driving a durable drop in cholesterol-raising PCSK9 and in LDL itself.

Sources: CRISPR Medicine News trial register · Genes, June 2026
Regulation

Medicine built for one person

Because the editing machinery is modular — swap the guide sequence, keep everything else — it's become possible to build a therapy for a single patient with a single rare mutation. The field calls this N-of-1 medicine, and it breaks the traditional trial model, which assumes a population to test on. In February 2026 the FDA announced a regulatory framework specifically for individualized genetic therapies, which is the kind of unglamorous administrative step that decides whether a technology actually reaches anyone.

Sources: Genes, June 2026 · Trends in Molecular Medicine, 2026
AI

Language models trained on genomes instead of English

Evo and its successor Evo 2 treat DNA as a language and learn it the way a chat model learns text. Evo 2 was trained on genomes from more than 100,000 species across the whole tree of life, reads up to a million nucleotides of context at once, and was published in Nature. It can flag disease-causing mutations in human genes and generate sequences at the scale of a simple bacterial genome. In validation work, AI-designed regulatory sequences were synthesized, inserted into mouse and human cells, and measurably changed how open or closed those regions of DNA were — meaning a generated sequence did something real in a living cell.

Sources: Arc Institute · BioPharmaTrend, March 2026
New RNA tools

Stitching, circles, and RNA-level switches

A recurring problem in gene therapy is that the delivery vehicles are too small to carry large genes. One answer, called StitchR, uses the cell's own repair pathways to fuse two RNA halves into one functional message, so a big protein can be delivered in two parts. A companion approach, CirculR, loops a strand into a circle, which makes it markedly more stable — useful for vaccines and therapies that need to persist. Separately, engineered Cas13 systems now let researchers degrade, block or boost specific transcripts, controlling gene activity at the RNA level rather than editing DNA at all.

Sources: University of Rochester Medicine · Nature
Synthesis & storage

Writing DNA, not just reading it

Reading genomes got cheap years ago. Writing them is the current frontier. The Synthetic Human Genome project, funded by Wellcome at £10 million over five years, isn't trying to build a whole human genome — the near-term goal is proof of concept via one fully synthetic human chromosome, with any synthetic DNA confined to lab cell lines, and a parallel social-science program on the ethics running alongside it. In a different direction entirely, the same synthesis capability is being used for archival data storage: DNA can hold enormous amounts of information in a very small, very stable volume, and the first commercial DNA storage cards reached market in early 2026.

Sources: Wellcome · Chemistry World · TechXplore

What the next decade probably looks like

Informed extrapolation, not prophecy. The honest summary is that the editing itself is close to solved and everything around it isn't.

Delivery is the real bottleneck

Getting the editing machinery into the right cells, in enough of them, without triggering an immune reaction, is harder than designing the edit. Liver and blood are relatively easy. Brain, muscle and lung are not. Expect most of the next decade's engineering effort to go here.

From treating to preventing

In-body editing of risk genes — cholesterol, clotting factors — points toward one-time preventive doses rather than lifelong daily pills. That reframes what a "drug" is, and nobody has worked out how to price or regulate a permanent one.

Design moves upstream to models

If a genomic model can predict which sequence produces which behavior, wet-lab work shifts from searching to confirming. The constraint becomes experimental validation throughput, and a lot of biology becomes an iteration loop that looks more like software.

RNA as a standing platform

Same manufacturing line, different sequence, different disease. That's already how vaccine production works, and it's spreading to therapeutics. It shortens development time dramatically and makes rapid response to new pathogens plausible.

The germline question won't stay quiet

Everything above edits body cells, so changes stop with the patient. Editing embryos would make changes heritable. It's broadly banned and broadly opposed, but the technical barrier keeps dropping, which means governance has to carry weight the technology no longer does.

Biosecurity gets harder

Cheap synthesis plus generative sequence design is genuinely dual-use. Screening what gets synthesized, and by whom, is an unsolved problem that the field is now taking seriously and publicly — the synthetic genome project built an ethics program in from day one for exactly this reason.
